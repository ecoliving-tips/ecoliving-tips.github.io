Common Chord Progressions Every Musician Should Know

What Is a Chord Progression?

A chord progression is a sequence of chords played in a specific order. While there are thousands of possible combinations, a handful of progressions appear in the vast majority of popular songs. Understanding these patterns lets you learn songs faster, write your own music, and improvise confidently.

Progressions are described using Roman numerals (I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, vii°) that represent the chord built on each scale degree. This system works in any key — the I chord is always the "home" chord, regardless of whether you're playing in C, G, or F#.

The Nashville Number System

Musicians use numbers instead of specific chord names so progressions can be discussed independent of key:

NumberIn Key of CIn Key of GQuality
ICGMajor
iiDmAmMinor
iiiEmBmMinor
IVFCMajor
VGDMajor
viAmEmMinor
vii°BdimF#dimDiminished

Capital numerals = major chords. Lowercase = minor chords. This pattern holds for every major key.

The 5 Most Common Progressions

1. I – V – vi – IV (The "Pop Progression")

In C: C – G – Am – F

In G: G – D – Em – C

This is arguably the most used progression in modern pop, rock, and worship music. It sounds uplifting and emotionally satisfying. The vi chord (minor) adds just enough tension before resolving back through IV to I.

Famous examples: "Let It Be" (Beatles), "No Woman No Cry" (Bob Marley), "With or Without You" (U2), "Someone Like You" (Adele), "Blessed Be Your Name" (Matt Redman)

Why it works: The progression creates a perfect emotional arc — confidence (I), tension (V), vulnerability (vi), hope (IV) — then cycles back. It never fully resolves until you return to I, which keeps listeners engaged.

2. I – IV – V – I (The "Classic" Progression)

In C: C – F – G – C

In G: G – C – D – G

The foundation of rock and roll, country, folk, and traditional hymns. Simple, strong, and universally satisfying. This is often the first progression beginners learn because it uses only three chords.

Famous examples: "Twist and Shout" (Beatles), "La Bamba" (Ritchie Valens), "Wild Thing" (The Troggs), "Amazing Grace" (traditional)

Why it works: The IV chord creates "subdominant" tension (wanting to move somewhere), the V chord creates "dominant" tension (strongly wanting to resolve), and the return to I provides complete resolution. It's the simplest tension-release cycle in Western music.

3. vi – IV – I – V (The "Sensitive" Progression)

In C: Am – F – C – G

In G: Em – C – G – D

Starting on the minor vi chord gives this progression a more emotional, introspective feel. It's the same four chords as #1, just starting from a different point in the cycle.

Famous examples: "Numb" (Linkin Park), "Africa" (Toto), "Zombie" (Cranberries), "Save Tonight" (Eagle-Eye Cherry)

Why it works: Beginning on a minor chord immediately establishes vulnerability. The progression then builds through IV and I before the V chord creates tension that drops back into the minor vi — creating an endless emotional loop.

4. I – vi – IV – V (The "Fifties" Progression)

In C: C – Am – F – G

In G: G – Em – C – D

The backbone of doo-wop, early rock and roll, and countless ballads. Moving from I directly to vi creates a nostalgic, bittersweet feeling that defined an era.

Famous examples: "Stand By Me" (Ben E. King), "Every Breath You Take" (The Police), "Unchained Melody" (Righteous Brothers), "Heart and Soul" (Hoagy Carmichael)

Why it works: The I-vi movement (from major to its relative minor) is one of the smoothest transitions in music — they share two of three notes. From there, IV and V provide the standard resolution cycle.

5. 12-Bar Blues (I – I – I – I – IV – IV – I – I – V – IV – I – V)

In A: A – A – A – A – D – D – A – A – E – D – A – E

The 12-bar blues is a 12-measure structure that forms the basis of blues, early rock, jazz, and R&B. Unlike other progressions that loop every 4 bars, this one tells a complete 12-bar story.

Famous examples: "Johnny B. Goode" (Chuck Berry), "Hound Dog" (Elvis Presley), "Red House" (Jimi Hendrix), "Sweet Home Chicago" (Robert Johnson)

Why it works: The 12-bar form mirrors the AAB lyric structure of blues poetry — state the problem (4 bars on I), restate it (4 bars starting on IV), resolve it (4 bars with V-IV-I). The final V chord acts as a "turnaround" that propels you back to the beginning.

Secondary Progressions Worth Knowing

ii – V – I (Jazz Standard)

In C: Dm – G – C

The most fundamental jazz progression. The ii chord "prepares" the V chord, which then resolves strongly to I. Almost every jazz standard contains multiple ii-V-I sequences.

I – V – vi – iii – IV – I – IV – V (Canon in D)

In D: D – A – Bm – F#m – G – D – G – A

Pachelbel's Canon progression has been borrowed by countless pop songs. Its 8-bar length and smooth voice leading create a stately, memorable framework.

I – bVII – IV – I (Mixolydian/Rock)

In A: A – G – D – A

Using the "flat seven" (bVII) chord borrowed from the Mixolydian mode gives rock music its characteristic swagger. Common in classic rock and indie.

How to Practice Progressions

  • Pick one progression and play it in the key of C (easiest shapes for guitar and piano)
  • Loop it — play the same 4 bars over and over until the changes become automatic
  • Transpose it — once comfortable in C, try G, then D, then A
  • Vary the rhythm — try different strumming patterns or picking styles
  • Sing over it — hum a melody while playing to develop your ear

    Recognizing Progressions by Ear

    Train your ear by listening for these patterns in songs you already know:

    • Sounds happy and uplifting? Probably I-V-vi-IV or I-IV-V
    • Sounds emotional from the start? Likely vi-IV-I-V or I-vi-IV-V
    • Has a 12-bar repeating structure? Almost certainly blues
    • Sounds jazzy with smooth resolution? Look for ii-V-I movement
